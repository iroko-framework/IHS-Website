Beneath the Archive Tree

On memory, custody, and the obligations that arise when living knowledge enters a permanent record
Guest Commentary · Archives & Stewardship · July 2026
By Amara N. Okafor

The first thing an archive does is accept. Before it describes, arranges, or provides access, it agrees to hold. That agreement is usually recorded as a transfer of custody, which makes it sound like a matter of shelving. It is not. What passes across the accession table is rarely just paper.

When a family brings in a photograph of a ceremony, they are not depositing an image. They are depositing a relationship, and they are trusting that the institution understands the difference between preserving an object and preserving the conditions under which the object can still be understood. Those are separate obligations, and only one of them is covered by a deed of gift.

The archive does not merely receive memory. It accepts an obligation to preserve the conditions under which memory may still be understood.

The record and the living voice

Oral testimony enters the record already transformed. Someone chose to speak, chose what to say, and chose what to hold back. A transcript flattens all three decisions into a single artifact that appears complete. The apparent completeness is the danger. A researcher fifty years from now will read the transcript and see a full account, not a negotiated one.1

This is not an argument against transcription. It is an argument for description that records the negotiation alongside the result: who was present, what was agreed, what was withheld and on whose instruction, and whether the speaker was told the recording would be public.

Translation without displacement

The same problem returns in translation. A term rendered into English acquires English assumptions, and the reader inherits them without being told. Where a word carries ritual weight, retaining the original and explaining it preserves more than an approximate equivalent ever will, even when the explanation is longer and less elegant.

An archive that translates everything into the language of its funders has not widened access. It has narrowed the record and called the result clarity.

A contribution to the record

None of this requires an institution to refuse material. It requires an institution to be honest in its description about what it holds, what it was told, and what it does not know. That honesty costs nothing at the point of accession and is nearly impossible to reconstruct later.

The tree in the courtyard outlives the people who gathered under it. That is the whole reason it was planted there. But a tree is not a record of what was said beneath it, and no archive should mistake the one for the other.
